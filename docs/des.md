# Discrete event simulation

## DES basics

### Definition

We can divide models into three different types:

1. People are discrete, time is continuous, and the model proceeds by jumping in time between events.
   These are called *discrete event simulations* (DES).
1. People are discrete, and time proceeds in discrete steps, like days.
   Many modeler's first experiences with discrete-person simulations are of this type.
1. The underlying state, like numbers of people in a disease status, is continuous.
   Time is also continuous.
   This category includes ODEs and PDEs.

### Components of a DES

1. *State*
1. *Clock*
1. *Event* list, also called plans, schedules, etc.
1. *Statistics*, also called reports, etc.
1. *Ending condition*, which can be thought of as an event

### Executing a DES

1. Initialize the state and population the first events
1. Advance time to the next event
1. Execute (or "resolve") that event.
   Typically this creates more events.
1. Repeat until the ending condition is met or there are no more events.

## Many epi models are simplifications of an underlying DES

Generation-by-generation models, like the chain binomial or a Galton-Watson branching process, are generated by a discrete event simulation, but we throw away the clock times of events and keep only the number of people infected per generation.

There is no single definition of a "compartmental" model, but for sake of argument consider models that reduce the possible state to counts of individuals in a small number of compartments.
This usually means reducing disease progression to a series of discrete states, like SIR.
It also means that we must not track the time any individual arrived in a compartment, so that all people in a compartment are identical.
This restricts the dwell times distributions to be memoryless.

ODE compartmental models can be formulated as the large-population limit of a DES, with memoryless dwell time distributions.

## Choosing event times

Implementing a DES requires explicit statements about the delays or distribution of delays between certain events.

If an event happens "once," then it is relatively easy to schedule: draw the delay time from the relevant distribution.
If an event happens multiple times, then we must use a *random process* to draw the inter-event times.
The most salient example is the times between subsequent infections (or infection attempts) from an infected person.

### Non-homogeneous renewal process

Given the time $t_0$ of the last event, we are interested in the distribution of the time $t$ of the next event.
In general, this follows an *non-homogeneous renewal process* (also called "time-dependent" or "age-dependent"):

$$
  f(t | t_0)
  = \lambda(t, t_0) \exp \left\{ -\int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s, t_0) \,ds \right\}
$$

where $\lambda(t, t_0)$ is the *hazard* of the next event.
Note that the probability that the next event will not have occurred by $t$ is:

$$
  S(t|t_0) \equiv 1 - \int_{t_0}^t f(s|t_0) \,ds
           = \exp \left\{ -\int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s, t_0) \,ds \right\}
$$

This satisfies the definition of hazard:

$$
  \lambda(t, t_0) = \frac{f(t|t_0)}{S(t|t_0)}
$$

The non-homogeneous renewal process is very general.
For example, it can allow the occurrence of an event to increase or decrease the probability of subsequent events.
The classic example is the [Hawkes process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawkes_process).
A non-homogeneous renewal processes can also allow for mathematically simple cases, such as exponentially-distributed times between events, if $\lambda(t, t_0)$ is a constant.
Or, it could allow for times between events to be some fixed interval $T$, if $\lambda(t, t_0) = \delta(t - t_0 - T)$.
Or, it could require that an event occur only once, if $\lambda(t, t_0) = 0$ for $t > t_0$.
(By convention, we use $\lambda(t,t)$ for the time of the first event.)

### Non-homogeneous Poisson process

It is convenient to restrict ourselves to *Markovian* non-homogeneous renewal processes, for which the hazard depends on the current time $t$ but not the time since the last event $t_0$:

$$
  f(t|t_0) = \lambda(t) \exp\left\{ -\int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s) \,ds \right\}
$$

This is an *non-homogeneous Poisson process* (NHPP; sometimes called a "point process" to emphasize we are interested in discrete events).
Because the hazard now depends only on clock time, and not the time since the last event, it is also called an event *rate*.

NHPPs have a number of important properties:

1. **Poisson distribution**: The number of events between times $t_0$ and $t$ is $N(t_0,t) \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\left[ \int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s) \,ds \right]$.
1. **Independent increments**: For $(t_0, t_1)$ and $(t_2, t_3)$ disjoint, $N(t_0, t_1)$ is statistically independent of $N(t_2, t_3)$.
1. **Superposition**: The events from two NHPPs, with rates $\lambda_1(t)$ and $\lambda_2(t)$, together form another NHPP, with rate $\lambda_1(t) + \lambda_2(t)$.
1. **Thinning**: An NHPP, with rate $\lambda(t)$, combined with a multinomial random variable that assigns each event in that process to one of $m$ discrete categories with probabilities $\pi_1(t), \pi_2(t), \ldots, \pi_m(t)$, together form $m$ independent NHPPs with rates $\pi_1(t) \lambda(t), \ldots, \pi_m(t) \lambda(t)$.

The Markovian property (and corollary independent increments property) allow us to "restart" sampling from any valid state.
In a DES, whatever the current clock time is, I can draw the next time, regardless of whether I am "at" an event or not.

The superposition and thinning properties allow for practical sampling methods.

### Aside: homogeneous Poisson processes

Many people use "Poisson process" to mean *homogeneous Poisson process*, for which the rate $\lambda(t)$ is a time-invariant constant $\lambda$.
For homogeneous Poisson processes, inter-event times are independent and exponentially distributed, with mean $1/\lambda$.
These are straightforward to sample from.

### Illustration: flipping coins

We have a NHPP with rate $\lambda(t)$ and want to sample events between time $t_0$ and $t_1$.
To do this, sample the total number of events $N(t_0,t_1) \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\left[ \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \lambda(s) \,ds \right]$, then distribute those events in time according to the normalized rate $\frac{\lambda(t)}{\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \lambda(s) \, ds}$.
This is equivalent to dividing $T = t_1 - t_0$ into a large number $m$ of small slices: $(t_0, t_0 + T/m), (t_0 + T/m, t_0 + 2T/m), \ldots, (t_1 - T/m, t_1)$.
If the slices are small enough, then the Poisson-distributed number of events in each slice will be zero or one.
The slices are small enough that we can get the event times to arbitrary precision.

Note the similarity to how the Poisson distribution arises from the binomial distribution, as the limit of flipping a large number $m \to \infty$ of coins, with diminishing per-flip probability $p \to 0$, such that the expected number $mp$ of heads remains fixed.

### Sampling non-homogeneous Poisson processes

If $\lambda(s)$ is known, and the cumulative probability

$$
  F(t|t_0) \equiv \int_{t_0}^t f(s|t_0) \,ds
           = 1 - \exp \left\{ -\int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s) \,ds \right\}
$$

is analytically tractable, then you can use *inverse transform sampling*.
Draw $U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0, 1]$, and compute the sampled time $t = F^{-1}(U|t_0)$.

In practice, this approach is not useful on its own.
We are typically performing a discrete event simulation precisely because we do not know $\lambda(t)$ is advance.
We normally need to combine this approach with *rejection sampling* or *rescheduling*.

### Rejection sampling

The superposition and thinning properties of NHPPs permit *Ogata's thinning algorithm*: to sample from a NHPP with rate $\lambda(t)$, given a *majorizing rate* $\lambda^\star(t)$, such that $\lambda^\star(t) > \lambda(t)$ over the relevant range, sample the next event from the NHPP defined by $\lambda^\star(t)$, then accept each event with probability $\lambda(t) / \lambda^\star(t)$.
Note that the majorizing NHPP is a superposition of the "accept" NHPP, with rate $\lambda(t)$, and a "reject" NHPP, with rate $\lambda^\star(t) - \lambda(t)$.
This is an example of a 2-category thinning.

The art here is the selection of a wise majorizing rate, which must be high enough to be majorizing, to ensure the validity of the sampling, but not so high as to lead to many rejections, which is inefficient.
In practice, this approach is useful for rates that may decrease below some initial rate function, such as transmission rates in the presence of face masks, isolation, or vaccination.

The majorizing rate is also called the "dominating," "forecasting," or "candidate" rate.

### Rescheduling

In some cases, we may have a rate $\lambda^\star(t)$ that is majorizing, but then something happens in the simulation to change the state, so that that rate is no longer majorizing.
For example, a infected person's behavior might change, increasing their contact rate, or they might take an antiviral that causes rebound.

In these situations, we can use the *rescheduling* algorithm.
Draw events according to the majorizing rate $\lambda^\star(t)$.
If you draw a time $t$ and discover that the majorizing rate ceased to be majorizing as of some *modification time* $t_m < t$, then advance the simulation, not to $t$, but to $t_m$, adjust the majorizing rate, and begin sampling again.

Note that this is permissible because the probability density of the next event time $t$ after the last event time $t_0$, conditioned on $t > t_m$, is the density of the next event time as if the last event were at $t_m$.
In a slight abuse of notation, let $T$ represent the random variable of the next event:

$$
  P(T < t | t_0, T > t_m) = \frac{P(t_m < T < t|t_0)}{P(T > t_m|t_0)}
                          = \frac{\int_{t_m}^t f(s|t_0) \,ds}{1 - F(t_m|t_0)}
$$

Now, take the time derivative:

$$
  \begin{align*}
    f(t|t_0,t > t_m) & = \frac{d}{dt} P(T < t | t_0, T > t_m)                                                                                        \\
                     & = \frac{f(t|t_0)}{1 - F(t_m|t_0)}                                                                                             \\
                     & = \frac{\lambda(t) \exp\left\{ \int_{t_0}^t \lambda(s) \,ds \right\}}{\exp \left\{ \int_{t_0}^{t_m} \lambda(s) \,ds \right\}} \\
                     & = \lambda(t) \exp\left\{ \int_{t_m}^t \lambda(s) \,ds \right\}                                                                \\
                     & = f(t|t_m)
  \end{align*}
$$

## Example: SIR

Thinning manifests in DESs as rejection of attempted events.
As an example, consider a DES of the classic SIR model:

- At model initiation, there are $N$ people, of whom $I(0)$ are infectious and the remainder are susceptible.
- Each person contacts other people, chosen at random from the whole population, at rate $\beta$.
  (Note that this rate is *extensive*.
  The contact rate from each person is fixed, so doubling $N$ doubles the number of contacts, rather than quadrupling it.)
- If an infectious person contacts a susceptible person, the susceptible person is becomes infected.
- Infections last for an exponentially-distributed time, with mean duration $1/\gamma$.

There are at least three ways to implement this model.

### One loop for the whole population

First, we could sample the time of the next contact in the entire population.
These events occur at rate $\beta N$.
At each event, we choose a candidate infector and infectee at random.
If the candidate infector is infectious and the candidate infectee is susceptible, infect the infectee.
Otherwise, reject the event.
Note that this means that, at timepoint $t$, the probability of accepting a randomly-selected candidate infector-infectee pair is $\frac{I(t)}{N} \cdot \frac{S(t)}{N - 1}$, so that the infection rate is $\beta \frac{S(t) I(t)}{N - 1}$, precisely as we expect.
In other words, we are sampling the NHPP with rate $\beta \frac{S(t) I(t)}{N - 1}$ using a majorizing NHPP with rate $\beta N$.

### One loop per infector

Second, rather than running one infection loop for the entire population, we could have separate loops for each infected person.
Upon a person's infection, we begin scheduling infection attempt events for that person, sampling times at rate $\beta$.
At each infection attempt, we first check if they have recovered.
If so, we reject the event and stop sampling for that person.
If they are still infectious, we draw a candidate infectee from the population at random and check if they are susceptible.
By the same logic as above, for each of the $I(t)$ infectors, we are sampling the NHPP with rate $\beta \frac{S(t)}{N - 1}$.
By the superposition property, we are sampling from the mathematically equivalent NHPP with a rate $\beta \frac{S(t) I(t)}{N - 1}$.

The mathematics of our majorizing rate is stuble.
Strictly speaking, the majorizing NHPP has rate $\beta \frac{S(t) I^\star(t)}{N - 1}$, where $I^\star(t)$ is the number of people who are either infectious or who have recovered but not yet had a rejected, post-recovery infection attempt.
The majorizing rate for each infector is:

$$
  \lambda^\star(t) = \begin{cases}
                       0     & t < t_I               \\
                       \beta & t_I < t < t_{R^\star} \\
                       0     & t_{R^\star} < t
                     \end{cases}
$$

where $t_I$ is the time this person is infected and $t_{R^\star}$ is the first sampled time after this person recovers.
Because $I^\star(t) \geq I(t)$, this rate is still majorizing.
Thus, in this implementation, the sampling rate is $\beta I^\star(t)$.

### Sample only susceptible infectee candidates

Third, we have one infection loop per infector, but we schedule both the time of the next infection attempt as well as the candidate infectee.
At time $t_0$, we both draw a next infection attempt time $t$ at rate $\beta \frac{S(t_0)}{N - 1}$ and also select one of the $S(t_0)$ susceptibles.
At time $t$, we check if the candidate infector has not recovered and if the candidate infectee is still susceptible.
Now the sampling rate is $\beta \frac{S^\star(t) I^\star(t)}{N - 1}$, where $S^\star(t) > S(t)$ is related to the numbers of susceptibles at the previous times before $t$ that the infection attempts were scheduled.

### Comparison of approaches

All three implementations accept events at rate $\beta \frac{S(t) I(t)}{N - 1}$, but they sample events at different rates: $\beta N$, $\beta I^\star(t)$, and $\beta \frac{S^\star(t) I^\star(t)}{N - 1}$.
The later implementations will be more efficient because they sample closer to the target rate and so have fewer rejected events.

The tradeoff is that those implementations are less flexible.
In the first example, the contact rates are wholly independent of disease progression, while the third samples events on the assumption that the number of susceptibles is non-increasing.
If the model were changed to an SIS model, for example, the sampling scheme for the third implementation would no longer be valid.
